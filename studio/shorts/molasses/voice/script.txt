In nineteen nineteen, a wave of molasses flooded the streets of Boston, and it moved faster than you can run. It's January, and by the harbor stands a steel tank, fifty feet tall, holding two point three million gallons of molasses. The man in charge couldn't read a blueprint. To test it, they poured in six inches of water. It leaked from day one, so the company painted it brown. Local kids came with buckets to scoop up the drips. Then a ship tops it up, a warm spell thaws the frozen city, and the tank starts to groan. Just after lunch, rivets shoot out like machine-gun fire, and the whole thing bursts. A brown wave twenty-five feet high rolls out at thirty-five miles an hour, bending the railway and flattening buildings. Luckily, the day was warm, so the molasses was runny. Unluckily, the day was warm, so the molasses was runny. Think of honey. Warm, it pours. Straight from the fridge, it's glue. The runny wave raced, then the freezing night set it like cold honey, holding people where they stood. Twenty-one people died. The company blamed anarchists with a bomb. The court blamed the tank. After that, Boston demanded a real engineer sign the plans. That molasses was meant for making alcohol. The very next day, America voted to ban alcohol. People still say "slow as molasses in January." Boston knows better.
