## Facts (with sources)
- 15 January 1919, about 12:40 pm, Commercial Street, North End, Boston. A steel tank of the Purity Distilling Co.
  (owned by US Industrial Alcohol) burst. 50 ft tall, 90 ft wide, holding about 2.3 million US gallons
  (~13,000 short tons) of molasses. (Wikipedia "Great Molasses Flood"; NPR 2019-01-15; Mass Moments)
- Wave about 25 ft high at its peak, about 35 mph. 21 people killed, about 150 injured. (Wikipedia; NPR; Boston.com 2017)
- The temperature had jumped from ~2 °F days before to above 40 °F that day; a recent delivery had filled the tank
  near capacity. Witnesses heard a rumble and a machine-gun rattle as the rivets shot out. (Wikipedia)
- The wave buckled girders of the Atlantic Avenue elevated railway and crushed buildings. (Wikipedia)
- Arthur P. Jell, the USIA treasurer in charge of the build, had no engineering background and could not read plans
  or blueprints; no engineer was consulted; he "tested" the 50 ft tank with only six inches of water. The tank leaked
  from the start and was painted brown (molasses colour) to hide it. Local children came with pails to collect the
  leaking molasses. (Stephen Puleo, *Dark Tide*, 2003, as quoted by CBN and in Puleo's interviews; Straight Dope; Mental Floss)
- That night the temperature fell below freezing; molasses viscosity rose ~4x or more, and about half the victims died
  because they were stuck. (Sharp, Kennedy, Rubinstein, Harvard, APS DFD 2016; Live Science, CS Monitor, APS News)
- USIA claimed anarchists bombed the tank; after years of hearings the court-appointed auditor found the company
  responsible. Boston's Building Department then required plans and calculations to be signed by an architect or
  engineer. (Wikipedia; Today I Found Out; Mental Floss)
- The molasses was stored for making alcohol. The 18th Amendment (Prohibition) was ratified on 16 January 1919,
  the very next day. (National Archives / standard history)
- "Slow as molasses in January" is an old American saying.

## Script v2 (tightened after take 1 ran 82 s; same facts and jokes)
In 1919, a wave of molasses flooded the streets of Boston, and it moved faster than you can run.
It's January, and by the harbor stands a steel tank, fifty feet tall, holding two point three million gallons of molasses.
The man in charge couldn't read a blueprint.
To test it, they poured in six inches of water.
It leaked from day one, so the company painted it brown.
Local kids came with buckets to scoop up the drips.
Then a ship tops it up, a warm spell thaws the frozen city, and the tank starts to groan.
Just after lunch, rivets shoot out like machine-gun fire, and the whole thing bursts.
A brown wave twenty-five feet high rolls out at thirty-five miles an hour, bending the railway and flattening buildings.
Luckily, the day was warm, so the molasses was runny.
Unluckily, the day was warm, so the molasses was runny.
Think of honey. Warm, it pours. Straight from the fridge, it's glue.
The runny wave raced, then the freezing night set it like cold honey, holding people where they stood.
Twenty-one people died.
The company blamed anarchists with a bomb. The court blamed the tank.
After that, Boston demanded a real engineer sign the plans.
That molasses was meant for making alcohol. The very next day, America voted to ban alcohol.
People still say "slow as molasses in January." Boston knows better.
